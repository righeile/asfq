What to measure

Wall detection

The first, the default, sets the edge by where muscle runs out, which collagen cannot move, so a lesion keeps its place inside the wall. The second marks the external elastic lamina on a healthy wall, but it ends the media in the same quantity fibrosis is measured in: a focal patch inside the media ends the media at the patch and is then counted as adventitia, so the more collagen a stretch has, the less of it is counted. The two do not measure the same band — don't mix them within a cohort, and sections measured before this became the default were measured the other way.
A lesion is collagen standing this many robust deviations above this wall's own level. A healthy media returns none at 3, and should: its collagen is lamellar, running through the whole thickness, so the distribution is broad and unimodal with nothing focal in it. The readout says what the cut was and what the wall actually reached, so none is an answer you can check rather than a silence.

How the assembler decides where a tile goes

Every pair of tiles is scored at every possible offset at once, using masked normalised cross-correlation evaluated by FFT. The signal being correlated is optical density, high-pass filtered: blank slide has an optical density of zero, so empty background contributes nothing, and uneven illumination between snapshots is removed before matching rather than after.

A placement is believed only if three independent things agree:

  • correlation — how well the shared tissue matches;
  • peak ratio — how much better the winning offset is than the best offset far away from it. Two tiles overlapping along a long featureless structure produce a correlation ridge rather than a peak, and the ratio stays near 1. That is the assembler telling you it does not know, which is worth more than a confident wrong answer;
  • overlapping tissue area — a correlation over forty pixels can reach 1.0 by accident.

Why tiles get left out

Tiles are added one at a time, and before each one is accepted it is checked against every tile already placed that it would overlap — not just the one that proposed it. Two tiles claiming the same piece of slide must agree about what is there. If they do not, the placement is refused and the tile waits for a better offer, or is parked to the right of the mosaic for you to place by hand.

This is the whole reason a spanning-tree layout can look finished and be wrong: it uses one match per tile and never notices that following the chain around a loop has dropped one piece of specimen on top of another.

On the canvas every tile is outlined by how well it agrees with its neighbours: grey means nothing to look at, rust is middling, red is a tile contradicting a neighbour, with a red line drawn to the tile it disagrees with. A white outline means you have it selected, and a dashed one means it is locked.

Drag, then align

You supply the topology — which piece belongs roughly where, the part software gets wrong on self-similar tissue. Correlation then supplies the last few pixels, the part people get wrong. Press A after dragging: the tile is re-matched against its new neighbours within a small radius of where you put it, so your decision is refined, never overruled. Lock a tile (L) and nothing will move it again.

Selecting several pieces at once

Three tools share the canvas. Move (V) drags a tile or, with shift, its whole island. Rect (R) drags out a rectangle and takes every tile it touches. Lasso (Q) draws a freehand loop and takes every tile whose centre or corner falls inside it — a lasso is normally thrown round a cluster rather than traced along tile edges, so demanding full enclosure would catch nothing when the pieces are bigger than the gesture. Tick take only fully enclosed tiles when you do mean exactly the pieces inside the line.

While a selection tool is active, shift adds to the selection and alt subtracts, so a rough sweep can be corrected rather than redrawn. Pressing on a tile that is already selected drags the selection instead of starting a new region, so marquee-then-move needs no mode switch in between; space+drag pans whatever tool is active. E grows the selection to every tile in the islands it already touches, which is the quick way to grab a whole parked fragment.

Everything that acts on a selection acts on all of it: A aligns each selected tile against its neighbours, L locks or unlocks them together, the arrow keys nudge them together, and dragging any one of them moves the set.

⌘Z (or ctrl+Z) undoes any change to the layout, and ⇧⌘Z redoes it — drags, nudges, rotations, locks, alignments, an applied merge, and auto-stitch itself, up to eighty steps back. Only the layout is recorded: undo puts the pieces back where they were and leaves your view, selection and settings alone. Repeated nudges in quick succession collapse into one step so a held arrow key does not bury the history.

From sections to genotypes

The Batch tab takes a tray: one parent folder, one sub-folder per section. Scanning it reads each folder name for mouse, genotype, age, segment, slide and section, and says how many folders it could not read before anything runs. A metadata.csv beside them overrides the parser, so a mis-read never has to be fixed by renaming data.

Slices of one segment in one mouse are pooled by adding the integrals — total collagen over total muscle, total media area over total wall length. Averaging the section ratios instead would weight a short off-cut the same as a whole ring.

The plots put a dot on every animal over the group bars. With three or four mice a side, a bar and an error bar cannot tell you whether a difference is four animals agreeing or one animal carrying the group, and that is what a reader needs to see. Ask for a dot per section and the figure says so in red: sections within an animal are repeated measures, and a bar drawn over them is not an n. p is printed as a number rather than as stars — at these group sizes 0.04 and 0.06 are the same result.

Genotypes are compared across per-mouse values. Sections and slides are repeated measures of one animal, so treating them as independent inflates n and shrinks p for reasons that have nothing to do with the biology. The tables print n_mice beside n_sections so the two can never be confused.

What the wall analysis measures

Stains are separated into concentrations by colour deconvolution. Optical density is linear in dye, so concentration can legitimately be integrated over distance; a hue-and-saturation score cannot, because it confounds how much dye there is with how dark the field was.

  • collagen per unit length — the collagen concentration integrated through the wall thickness at each position, in OD·µm. It is comparable between vessels of different size, but it is not a fibrosis index: it integrates through the wall, so a thicker wall gives more of everything. On these sections it tracks media thickness at r = +0.97.
  • collagen ÷ muscle — the same quantity divided by muscle integrated through the same wall, so thickness cancels. This is the readout to quote. The bottom panel of the figure plots it position by position with the running value over it — collagen so far over muscle so far — which settles on the whole-wall ratio, so a drift away from that line is a real gradient along the vessel rather than a thick patch.
  • blue area fraction in the media — the fraction of the muscle layer where collagen outweighs muscle. This asks a question with no free parameter, which matters: collagen inside the media is unimodal, so an automatic threshold has nothing to lock onto. On one section, a change that moved the collagen percentiles by under 0.02 moved Otsu from 0.778 to 0.632 and the reported fibrosis from 9 % to 34 %. A threshold derived per section also makes sections incomparable, since a more fibrotic section earns a higher threshold that cancels part of the difference being measured.

The media runs from the luminal surface to where collagen overtakes muscle and stays ahead — the external elastic lamina. The crossover has to hold for several microns, so a fibrotic patch inside the media does not truncate the measurement of itself.
